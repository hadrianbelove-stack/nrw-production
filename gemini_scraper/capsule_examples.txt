SEED EXAMPLES
=============

These are condensed from Cinefamily calendar copy (2007-2011) to show the target
voice and tone. As the approved capsule bank grows, these will be supplemented
(and eventually outweighed) by your own approved rewrites.


Forbidden Zone (1980) -- Richard Elfman
Future composer Danny Elfman plays a singing and dancing Satan with a chorus line of ghouls in this key early-'80s midnight movie classic about a family who discovers a gateway to the Sixth Dimension inside their new house. With stark black-and-white cinematography, a fantastic fixation on retro Fleischer visuals, and freaky star turns by Susan Tyrrell and pint-sized Herve Villechaize, it plays like what happens when the Fleischer Brothers meet John Waters on peyote. Director Richard Elfman shot it with his Oingo Boingo bandmates on a shoestring and it's been a repertory staple ever since.

---

The Long Goodbye (1973) -- Robert Altman
In Elliot Gould, Robert Altman found the perfect performer to match his own trademark slinky narratives — Gould's Philip Marlowe is a man out of time, with an ethical code deeply at odds with hedonistic '70s L.A. From Sterling Hayden's drunken Bukowski-esque author to Henry Gibson's shady quack, everything about the film is strange, sly and close to perfection. The John Williams score, which keeps rearranging the same theme in wildly different styles, is its own kind of joke.

---

Little Murders (1971) -- Alan Arkin
A bitterly zany black comedy caked in post-'68 disillusionment, adapted from Jules Feiffer's stage play by first-time director Alan Arkin. Gould plays a shut-in photographer so resentful of his own success that he's turned to photographing feces, and somehow it works as a love story. Features Donald Sutherland as a hippie priest officiating what is easily the greatest wedding sequence in cinema history, plus Arkin himself as a hysterical detective.

---

The Navigator (1924) -- Buster Keaton
Keaton purchased a disused steamship on a whim and figured out only later how to build a feature around it — the result is a nonstop comedic tour de force about a buffoonish upper-cruster trapped on an adrift ocean liner with his reluctant sweetheart. The two learn to cope with uncooperative deck chairs and an attacking horde of cannibals with the kind of escalating physical invention that makes Keaton the greatest stunt performer who ever lived. Worth seeing on a big screen if you can manage it.

---

M*A*S*H (1970) -- Robert Altman
Easy Rider may have unlocked the gates of Hollywood prudishness but M*A*S*H dynamited the gate doors right off their hinges — Altman's overlapping dialogue, realistic war gore, and devil-may-care attitude were genuinely shocking in 1970. Gould and Sutherland complement each other's comedic timing with immaculate Marx Brothers flair as military surgeons trying to retain their sanity by any batty means necessary. The comedy is very much of its time, but the film's savage irreverence hasn't aged a day.

---

Seven Chances (1925) -- Buster Keaton
Features the most magnificently ludicrous premise in all of Keatondom: Buster is a broke broker who inherits $7 million on the condition he gets married before his 27th birthday — which happens to be today. After a series of wildly unsuccessful proposals he prints a personals ad, and what follows is one of Keaton's most stunning chase sequences, with hundreds of would-be brides barreling down the streets of Los Angeles. Capped off by the famous avalanche bit, a precisely constructed sketch that still makes audiences gasp.
